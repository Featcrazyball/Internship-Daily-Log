Compared to last week, where I spent most of my time outside the office, this 
week brought me back to the web application. However, this time it was different. 
Instead of only being the developer, I also became a user of the web application. 
On Wednesday, I used it to check a client’s documents for Cyber Essentials and 
Cyber Trust Mark eligibility. Apart from that, I started building a whole new section 
of the web application that uses AI to generate and edit network diagrams,
self-studied system design, and presented the basics of networking to my colleagues 
on Friday.

The most significant moment this week was using our own web application for a real client. 
Although the web application scanned through the clauses for me, it still took hours as 
there were many clauses. Honestly, this was quite humbling. As a developer, I had tested 
these features many times, but always with short sample documents. It was only when I used 
it for actual work that I felt how slow and repetitive it was. Thus, the very next day, I 
implemented improvements such as generating documents based on what is lacking, better 
recommendations and the ability to re-access specific clauses. To put it in simple terms, 
it is like a chef who never tastes his own food. He may follow the recipe perfectly, yet he 
will never know that it is too salty. This links back to what I learnt from InsiderSecurity 
in Week 6, where solutions should be built around what the customer actually needs. The same 
idea shaped the network diagram generator. Instead of expecting users to write complex 
technical prompts, I built a workflow where the AI guides them through simple questions.

Next, I would like to mention that I followed through on one of my action items from last week. 
In Week 8, I said I wanted to read further into distributed systems. On Monday, I used my available 
time to self-study cloud architecture and system design concepts, such as horizontal scaling, load 
balancing, caching and database replication. I then applied them to help revise the company’s system 
architecture across two iterations. This felt very rewarding, as it was the first time an action 
item I had written in a reflection turned into actual work.

On the other hand, not everything went smoothly. On Friday, I spent an hour trying to recreate a 
bug that another employee found, but I was unable to do so. In school, every bug I encountered was 
on my own machine, so I could always see it happen. In the workplace, the person who finds the bug 
and the person who fixes it are often different people, with different devices and data. Furthermore, 
my networking presentation, while well received, showed that I still have room to grow. My colleagues’ 
feedback was that it needed a clearer problem statement, a stronger summary and links to relevant incident 
reports. Looking back, I focused so much on explaining the concepts that I forgot to explain why the 
audience should care.

So, what have I learnt? Firstly, the best way to find the weaknesses of a product is to use it for real 
work. Secondly, self-study is only meaningful when it is applied, and my system design learning proved 
that. Thirdly, a presentation should start with a problem, not with the content. Coming from Montfort, 
I have always stuck by the motto “Age Quod Agis”, meaning do well in whatever you do, and this week reminded 
me that doing well also means looking at my work from the user’s and the audience’s point of view.

Moving forward, I will use every feature I build on a real or realistic document before marking it as complete. 
In addition, when a bug cannot be recreated, I will ask for the exact steps, browser, account and screenshots 
before spending time on it, and suggest adding better error logging to the web application. Finally, for my 
next presentation, I will open with a real incident as the problem statement and end with a summary slide, so 
that my colleagues know exactly what to take away.
