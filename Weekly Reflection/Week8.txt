This week, I spent much less time in front of my laptop than usual. On Wednesday, the other 
interns and I assisted Mr Yew Mun during his talk at the SME Centre@SCCCI. On Thursday, I 
accompanied him to a client’s office to conduct a network vulnerability assessment, and on 
Friday, I wrote two reports based on our findings, one for technical and another for non-technical 
stakeholders. In between, I implemented features on the web application, sent Mr Yew Mun a 
proposal on distributed systems and tested a Mudra Band that a colleague passed to me.

The first significant moment was the SME workshop. During Govware in Week 6, I had to work up 
the courage every time I approached a stranger. However, at the SME Centre, I noticed that I was 
much more comfortable networking, offering support to the audience and introducing our company. 
While I am still an introvert, this showed me that social skills can be trained just like technical 
skills. Furthermore, it built on the body language lessons from last week’s presentation, as I made a 
conscious effort to maintain eye contact and keep an open posture.

The second was the network vulnerability assessment. Until now, my work has mostly revolved around one web 
application. Being exposed to a real client’s network gave me a much broader view of how organisations 
actually set up their infrastructure, which is often very different from the clean lab environments I used at 
NYP. In addition, writing two separate reports for the same findings taught me that the same information must 
be presented differently depending on the audience. This connects directly to my Cybersecurity specialisation, 
where I learnt the theory but rarely had to explain findings to a real client.

The third was the Mudra Band, a wristband that reads signals from the wrist to control devices through finger gestures. 
Honestly, it was not very practical for daily use, as we currently work on 2D screens where a mouse and keyboard are 
faster. Nevertheless, I could see how useful it would be in the future when we move towards 3D interfaces, such as 
augmented and virtual reality, where there is no physical surface to type or click on. This reminded me that a technology 
should be judged by the problem it is meant to solve, not only by how well it fits today’s tools.

Lastly, the distributed systems proposal was a valuable experience. As the company acquires more customers, the web 
application will need to handle a heavier load. When I explained my proposal to Mr Yew Mun, I realised that putting my 
ideas into words forced me to fill gaps in my own understanding. Thus, articulating my thoughts to someone else helped
me understand them better myself.

So, what have I learnt? Firstly, my communication skills have improved, both when talking to strangers and when 
explaining technical ideas to my superior. Secondly, seeing a client’s network showed me that real-world environments 
are messier than what textbooks describe. Moving forward, I will continue volunteering for client-facing tasks, 
as they are where I grow the most. In addition, I plan to write down and explain my ideas to a colleague before 
proposing them, since this week proved that it sharpens my thinking. Finally, I would like to read further into 
distributed systems so that my next proposal is backed by stronger technical reasoning.
